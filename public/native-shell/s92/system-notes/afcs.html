AFCS: The Coupled Flight Director

AFCS: The Coupled Flight Director

Dual-redundant SAS, Attitude Hold, and Coupled Flight Director — every basic mode's engagement logic, power limiting, and what happens automatically on an engine failure while coupled.

Source: S-92A RFM Part 2, Section I, Chapter 16 (AFCS — Introduction, Operation, Sensors, Servo Actuators, Flight Control Computers, SAS, AP, Coupled Flight Director, Cockpit Controls, Pilot Interface, CFD Mode Engagement, Basic Modes, Navigation Modes, Approach Modes).

The architecture

The AFCS is dual-redundant in every feature except the trim actuators, built from sensors (rate gyros, dual AHRS, accelerometers, RVDTs, ADCs, radar altimeter, nav radios, FMS, FADEC), two Flight Control Computers (FCCs — each with an independent SAS section and AP section, separate sensors and power supplies), and servo actuators (trim, SAS, boost).

Three layers stack on top of each other. SAS provides basic rate damping — it senses a disturbance (a gust-induced nose pitch-up, say) and makes small, immediate corrections without trying to return the aircraft to its original attitude. AP adds attitude hold on top of SAS: it remembers the trimmed pitch/roll/yaw attitude and actively works to regain it after a disturbance, plus hover augmentation, gust alleviation, and heading hold (below 50 KIAS) or heading hold with turn coordination (above 50 KIAS). The Coupled Flight Director (CFD) sits on top of both, using the trim actuators to fly a pilot-selected path hands-off.

SAS servos vs. trim actuators — what actually moves the stick

Two sets of four hydraulic SAS servos (eight total, two per axis) make control inputs without moving the cockpit controls — each servo has 5% control authority, combining to 10% total per axis. If a primary SAS computer or its sensors fail, the pilot can select secondary SAS, in which the AP computer (using AHRS data) takes over that side.

Four electromechanical trim actuators, by contrast, do move the cockpit controls when they move — they provide control referencing and an artificial control gradient, each with 100% control authority but limited to 10%/second rate. Once trimmed, any control movement without releasing trim builds an increasing force back toward the reference. Trim actuators are what the AP and CFD use for their control inputs.

The CFD will not couple to pitch or roll below 50 KIAS — radar altitude hold is the exception and can engage at any airspeed. Two-cue vs three-cue: whether vertical speed/altitude is held via the pitch axis (two cue) or the collective axis (three cue) changes which beeper does what — check the PFD's P/R/C axis annunciations to know which is active.

Power limiting

When coupled to the collective axis, the CFD automatically limits power to maximum continuous torque/TGT/Ng. On a power limit, the CFD trades airspeed to hold the desired altitude or climb rate, working toward bucket airspeed (minimum power required speed — 80 KIAS at sea level, decreasing 1 kt per 1,000 ft). If the aircraft reaches bucket speed and still can't hold the target, it will instead descend (in ALT) or reduce climb rate (in VS), with a yellow CHECK POWER advisory and a single aural alert.

Power limiting automatically shifts from maximum-continuous to takeoff power levels when descending below 200 ft AGL, reverting once the aircraft climbs back above 300 ft AGL.

Engine failure while coupled

The pilot should always take manual control and fly the appropriate emergency procedure — but the CFD's automatic behavior in the interim is worth knowing. Coupled to the collective (three-cue): the CFD automatically lowers collective to restore Nr to 105% and stay below maximum continuous power, resetting the airspeed reference to bucket speed. Coupled to altitude/VS on the pitch axis (two-cue): the aircraft tries to hold by reducing airspeed; if power still isn't sufficient and airspeed drops below 50 KIAS, it auto-decouples — take control before decelerating below bucket speed. Airspeed coupled to pitch with no vertical mode selected: the airspeed reference resets to bucket speed automatically. If both engines fail while coupled, the collective axis auto-decouples.

Approach modes

The CFD flies VOR, FMS, ILS, Pseudo-ILS (P-ILS), and Localizer Back Course approaches, all selectable in two-cue or three-cue — three-cue is recommended for all approaches since two-cue has no deceleration feature. VOR, basic FMS, and localizer approaches have no automatic vertical/airspeed function; the pilot can layer in altitude preselect, VS, or airspeed hold manually.

On an ILS with GS and DCL armed, the CFD flies a pre-programmed deceleration to 80 KIAS by 200 ft AGL, continuing to 60 KIAS by 50 ft AGL, leveling at 50 ft AGL if GS is selected — the radar altimeter must be valid for DCL to function, and changing the airspeed reference while DCL is armed will de-arm it. P-ILS lets the CFD carry ILS-style glideslope, deceleration, and level-off features on an FMS-based approach, transitioning automatically from FMS flight plan to FMS approach to P-ILS as each phase is intercepted.

VOR station passage uses DME (switching at 1.5 DME, or 1.3 in VOR approach mode) or, without DME, needle-swing rate to detect the station and smooth the transition to the outbound course — the green NAV annunciation flashes throughout. A noisy or unreliable VOR signal can cause false 'S' turns or a false station-passage detection; monitor raw VOR data and deselect NAV if the course indicator moves erratically.
